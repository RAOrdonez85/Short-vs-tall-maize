About this page

The plants and roots are drawn from field measurements of short-stature (brachytic, br2) and conventional tall maize grown at Purdue research farms in 2018, 2019, 2024 and 2025 under different nitrogen rates. Roots were cored to 120 cm at the R2 stage, in and between the rows following the sampling protocol of Ordóñez et al. (2018, European Journal of Agronomy), and the profile is shown to that depth. The rooting front deepens over the season at the root front velocities measured for maize in the US Midwest (Ordóñez et al., 2018).

Root length in each soil layer, root biomass, above-ground biomass, plant height and the position of the leaves, ear, tassel and brace roots follow the measured traits of each stature. The reference ear is 20 cm long with 16 rows of 35 kernels; across sites and N rates the kernels per row and ear length follow the measured grain per plant and kernel weight. The ear elongates rapidly but stays thin in the 15 days before silking, carrying about 1,200 florets (potential kernels); during the lag phase after silking most apical florets abort and only the kernels set from the field data keep growing. After silking, kernels stay at the watery stage for about 200 °C·d before filling, and kernels get smaller from the base to the tip of the ear, following field measurements of ear growth around silking and of grain weight along the ear (Ordóñez et al., 2015, 2018). The exact shape of each root and leaf is illustrative.

Built on original field research. This model builds on the Corn Roots by N Rate model, which is grounded in unpublished field data: real plant and root measurements taken at multiple points across the growing season, under several nitrogen rates. The short- and tall-stature plants here use that model's structure and parameters for how roots and shoots develop over time, combined with field measurements of each stature. The result captures how plants actually develop through the season, not just how they look at a single sampling date.

Beyond maize. The same approach can be applied to other grain crops and legumes. It provides a foundation for studying plant processes and developmental changes more precisely, and for strengthening digital twin platforms focused on plant phenotypes, where models must reproduce real, measured plant behavior.

Let's collaborate. Dr. Raziel A. Ordóñez welcomes partnerships to extend this work to new crops, hybrids, environments and management practices, and to turn field data into tools for research, teaching and decision making.
